What this actually gets you

A deficit of about 450 kcal a day → roughly 0.4 kg per week. From 77.5 kg that is ~74 kg by mid-October — the weight you felt strongest at — while lifting three times a week so what you lose is fat and what stays is muscle.

Going faster is possible and a bad idea. Below about 1700 kcal you lose muscle, sleep gets worse, and with a 3-year-old in the house you cannot afford either.

The three rules that do 90% of the work

  • Protein first, every meal. If a meal has 35–45 g of protein in it, the rest tends to take care of itself. This is the hardest part of being vegetarian in a deficit, and the most important.
  • Do not drink calories. Juice, sodas, the second beer, the oat-milk latte with syrup. Coffee, tea, water, and milk with protein in it are fine.
  • Eat the same breakfast and lunch on weekdays. You control those two meals; make them automatic so you have zero decisions to make and dinner can be whatever the family is eating.
The 71 kg trap. You said you sit at 71 kg when you are not training. That is not the goal here. 74 kg with muscle looks and feels completely different from 71 kg without it. Judge this by the mirror, your belt, and what you lift — not by the scale alone.

Weighing yourself

Three mornings a week, straight after the toilet, before eating. Track the weekly average in the Progress tab of your training log, never a single day. Daily swings of ±1 kg are water and food volume, not fat.

If the weekly average has not moved in three consecutive weeks, take 150 kcal off — roughly one slice of rugbrød with topping, or a handful of nuts.

Hitting 140 g means roughly four protein anchors a day. Since you are open to protein powder, one of those four can take thirty seconds — that is the single biggest convenience lever you have.

Build a meal

No fixed menu. Pick one from each slot and you have a meal that lands near target — whatever is in the fridge, whatever the family is eating.

The four slots

1 · Protein anchor — pick one, non-negotiable

250 g skyr · 200 g hytteost · 200 g kvark · 3 eggs · 150 g firm tofu · 100 g tempeh · 240 g tin of chickpeas or beans · 150 g edamame · 80 g halloumi · 30 g protein powder

2 · Base — pick one, this is your portion control

2 slices rugbrød · 60 g dry pasta · 150 g cooked rice · 200 g potatoes · 50 g oats · 60 g dry couscous or bulgur · 1 large tortilla

3 · Vegetables — as much as you like, genuinely

Whatever is cheap and looks good. Frozen counts. Tinned tomatoes count. A bag of spinach wilted into anything counts. There is no wrong answer in this slot and no reason to measure it.

4 · Flavour — pick one, keep it to one

15 g tahini · 10 g olive oil · 20 g pesto · 15 g peanut butter · 30 g feta · 30 g grated cheese · 3 tbsp skyr-and-garlic sauce

One from each slot lands at roughly 500–650 kcal and 30–40 g protein. Three of those a day plus one snack is the whole plan. That is all the arithmetic there is.

Why a system instead of a menu. A 7-day menu assumes a week that goes to plan. Yours will not — a nap collapses, someone gets a cold, a seminar runs late. A slot system survives all of that, because the decision at 18:40 stops being “what was I supposed to cook” and becomes “which protein do I have”.

Why breakfast carries so much weight here. If you land 35 g of protein before you leave the house, you only need ~105 g across two meals you control less. Miss breakfast and you are chasing 140 g from lunch and dinner alone, which for a vegetarian is genuinely hard.

If you eat in the canteen

  • Take double of the vegetarian protein element — the eggs, the beans, the cheese, the tofu.
  • Fill half the plate with vegetables before you touch the starch.
  • One portion of the carb, not two. Skip the bread basket.
  • Keep a tub of skyr or a shaker with a scoop of powder in your bag. It closes the protein gap on any canteen lunch, and canteens are where vegetarian options are usually lowest in protein.

The no-cooking desk lunch

Rugbrød · 200 g hytteost · 2 boiled eggs · cucumber, tomato, plenty of pepper. ~40 g protein, zero preparation. Boil a batch of eggs on Monday and keep them in the office fridge.

Dinner — eat what the family eats

You are not cooking a separate meal. You are adding one slot to the meal that already exists.

The add-on principle

Family dinners tend to be carb-heavy and protein-light — pasta, rice, pancakes, potatoes. That is fine. Take a smaller portion of the base and add one protein anchor:

  • 150 g tofu, pan-fried while the pasta boils — 6 minutes, one pan
  • 2 eggs, fried or boiled, on top of whatever it is
  • A tin of beans or lentils stirred into your portion only
  • 200 g skyr for dessert, with berries — it counts
  • 40 g grated cheese on your half of the dish

Every one of these is under five minutes and none requires a second recipe.

The leftovers problem. Finishing what the 3-year-old leaves on the plate is, for most parents, several hundred invisible calories a day — easily the whole deficit. Scrape it into the bin or into a container for tomorrow. This one habit is worth more than any single change on this page.

Two gaps to cover, since there is no fish or meat

Nothing about a lacto-ovo vegetarian diet stops you building muscle — the protein table handles that. But two nutrients are genuinely harder to get, and both matter for training:

  • Omega-3 (EPA/DHA). Almost absent without fish. Flax and walnuts supply ALA, which the body converts poorly. An algae-oil supplement (250–500 mg EPA+DHA/day) closes it directly. Relevant to joint comfort, which matters with a shoulder you are rehabbing.
  • Iron. Plant iron absorbs poorly on its own. Eat the iron sources — lentils, beans, spinach, tofu — with vitamin C (peppers, tomato, citrus, broccoli), which multiplies absorption several-fold. Avoid tea and coffee with those meals; they do the opposite.

If you ever do want the occasional meat or fish meal, nothing here breaks — it just replaces one anchor. But the plan does not need it.

Shopping & the 40-minute Sunday

The standing list

Fridge: plain skyr (the big tub) · hytteost · kvark · eggs (12+) · firm tofu · tempeh · halloumi · feta · skummetmælk · a bag of spinach

Cupboard: tinned chickpeas · tinned white and kidney beans · red lentils · tinned tomatoes · oats · quinoa or bulgur · couscous · pasta · peanut butter · tahini · protein powder

Freezer: frozen berries · edamame · frozen spinach · frozen stir-fry mix · frozen rye bread

Fresh: whatever vegetables look good and are cheap. There is no wrong answer here.

Forty minutes on Sunday

  • Boil 8 eggs. They live in the fridge and solve half the week’s protein emergencies.
  • Bake 2 blocks of pressed, cubed tofu with soy, garlic and paprika — 25 min at 200°C, mostly unattended.
  • Cook a big pot of quinoa or bulgur, and a batch of red lentil dahl to freeze in portions.
  • Make one jar of sauce (tahini-lemon or skyr-garlic-dill). Sauce is what stops meal prep tasting like meal prep.

Four things. After that the weekday meals are assembly, not cooking.

Supplements — short list

  • Vitamin D — genuinely necessary in Denmark from October to April. 20–25 µg/day.
  • B12 — important without meat or fish, even with dairy and eggs. Cheap, take it.
  • Algae-based omega-3 — 250–500 mg EPA+DHA/day. The one supplement your diet most clearly needs.
  • Protein powder — not magic, just a convenient 24 g of protein for 120 kcal. Whey isolate or a pea/rice blend, whichever you prefer the taste of.
  • Creatine, 5 g/day — optional but higher value for you than for most: vegetarians start with lower muscle creatine, so the effect is larger. Well studied, safe, ~50 kr/month.

Nothing else. Fat burners and BCAAs are a tax on the hopeful.

Logging meals from photos

Yes, this works — and it needs no extra app.

How it works

  • Snap the plate before you eat. Phone camera, one photo, two seconds.
  • Drop it into the Fitness chat with Claude, with a word of context if it helps (“that’s a whole tin of chickpeas”, “half of that went to my son”).
  • You get back an estimate of calories and protein, and a running daily total.
  • End of the week: a summary of where the protein actually landed and what to change.

Send several photos at once if you prefer — a whole day in one go works fine.

What it is good at, and what it is not

Good: protein estimates, portion sanity-checks, spotting the invisible calories (oil, cheese, the toddler’s leftovers), and telling you whether a day was roughly on target.

Not good: precision. A photo estimate is comfortably ±15–20%. Anyone claiming better, app or human, is guessing with more confidence.

That accuracy is fine for what you need. You are not medicating a condition; you are steering a weekly average. The scale and the tape measure keep you honest — photos just tell you why they are doing what they are doing.

Do not log every meal forever. Do it properly for two weeks so you learn what 40 g of protein and 550 kcal actually look like on a plate. After that, photograph only the meals you are unsure about, and the weeks where progress has stalled. Tracking is a tool for calibration, not a permanent condition — and with a small child at home, anything that demands daily perfection will be the first thing to break.

A good week looks like

  • 3 gym sessions (2 is still a good week)
  • Protein at or above 130 g on at least 5 days
  • Two “off-plan” meals, eaten without any guilt whatsoever — they are in the budget
  • 8,000+ steps most days
  • Weekly average weight down 0.3–0.5 kg

Four out of five is a successful week. Insisting on five out of five every week is how people quit in November.

Estimates use the Mifflin–St Jeor equation with an activity factor for a sedentary job plus three resistance sessions per week. Individual variation is real — treat these numbers as a starting point to be corrected by what the scale does over three weeks, not as a measurement.
